# Interview — Clinic Nurse on Reading Scribe-Drafted Visit Notes

## Objective
Two months after the ambient scribe went GA in January 2026, understand how a clinic nurse works from visit notes the scribe drafted for someone else. The scribe drafts visit notes for physicians, NPs and PAs only; nurses don't use it for their own documentation, and nurse charting is still manual. Earlier ambient-scribe research tested the people who dictate and sign the note. This session asked about the people who read the signed note afterwards and act on it, and whether the February 2025 nursing survey's top concern ("losing my clinical narrative voice") means the same thing to a nurse who now reads scribe-drafted notes every day.

## Method
- **Method:** 1:1 interview, 45 min
- **Researcher:** Priya Patel
- **Full participant roster:** see `participants.md` in this folder
- **Context:** The participant is a registered nurse in an ambulatory primary care clinic, mid-career. After a visit they make callbacks (results, medication questions), give patients follow-up instructions and act on orders, all from the provider's signed visit note, which since GA is often scribe-drafted. They don't use the scribe themselves. They did not take part in any earlier session, including the February 2025 nursing survey; they are not P41, the outpatient clinic nurse quoted in that survey.

## Key Findings
- **[HIGH]** *(plan section)* What they act on is the plan section: the follow-up interval, the medication change, what to tell the patient. In scribe-drafted notes the plan is usually there, but sometimes part of it is said in the conversation and written up in the body of the note rather than the plan, so they now read the whole note before a callback instead of going straight to the plan. They described this as "a few times a week" an instruction they almost missed; that is their informal impression, not a count. If the plan is incomplete, they message the provider before calling the patient, which delays the callback.
- **[MEDIUM]** *(voice)* For them, the "voice" concern is about other people's notes, not their own. They can tell which notes are scribe-drafted because they read more like a conversation and less like the provider they know, and that makes it harder to tell what the provider actually meant to emphasise. They did not worry about their own voice, because the scribe doesn't draft nursing notes. This complicates the February 2025 survey's finding, which framed the concern as nurses losing their own narrative voice.
- **[MEDIUM]** *(trust)* They don't know which parts of a signed note the provider edited and which the scribe drafted, and they assume the provider read it all. They said they would not change how they act on a note if they knew, but they would know where to ask questions. This is a stated expectation; no alternative view was shown.

## Representative Quotes
> "I live in the plan. If it said 'come back in two weeks' in the conversation and the plan doesn't say it, the patient's going to ask me and I won't know."
> — Registered Nurse (primary care clinic), P138

> "It's not my voice I'm worried about, the scribe doesn't write my notes. It's that I can't hear the doctor in theirs anymore."
> — Registered Nurse (primary care clinic), P138

## Recommendations
1. Share with the ambient scribe team that nurses act on the plan section of scribe-drafted notes, so follow-up intervals, medication changes and patient instructions said during the visit need to reach the plan, not only the body.
2. When the survey's "voice" concern is next cited, say whose voice: for this nurse it was the provider's, in notes the nurse reads but didn't write.

## Follow-ups / Open Questions
- How often plan items land outside the plan section in scribe-drafted notes is not measured; "a few times a week" is one nurse's impression.
- Whether other clinic nurses read the whole note or only the plan is not known; this is one interview.
- Whether nurses would use a marker of which parts the provider edited is untested; no such marker exists.
